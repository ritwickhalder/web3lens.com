Crypto moves through video. Protocol upgrades are explained in hour-long community calls. Founders answer questions in AMAs. Analysts walk through on-chain data on camera. Following all of it is a full-time job, and most of those hours contain a few minutes you actually need. This guide shows how to use [SummaryTube](https://summarytube.com/), an AI summary extension for YouTube, to find those minutes quickly, and how to use Web3Lens to check every claim you hear against the chain.

## Why video is hard to research

Written sources can be skimmed, searched and quoted. Video cannot, unless you give it those properties:

- **Length.** A 90-minute governance call or a four-hour podcast does not fit into a working day.
- **Density varies.** Much of it is introductions, tangents and sponsor reads.
- **Claims are spoken, not linked.** A presenter says "this wallet moved 40 million" and moves on. There is no link to check.
- **Hype travels fast.** Clips get shared without context, and context is exactly what a summary restores.

The goal is not to skip understanding. It is to decide *where* to spend your attention.

## What SummaryTube gives you

SummaryTube adds a panel beside the YouTube player for Chrome, Firefox and Edge. For a video it provides:

- **A summary** with the main points in readable sections.
- **Key moments** with timestamps, so you can jump to the part you care about.
- **Q&A**, where you ask questions about the video and get answers grounded in what was said.
- **A transcript** you can search and copy.

## A workflow: from video to verified facts

This is a practical routine for any crypto video that makes factual claims.

### 1. Summarize first, then decide

Open the video and read the summary before you press play. Within a minute you know whether it covers a topic you care about and whether it contains concrete, checkable statements. If it is mostly opinion and price talk, you have saved the hour.

### 2. Jump to the key moments

For videos worth your time, use the key moments to go straight to the sections about the subject: a contract change, a treasury move, a governance vote. Watch those parts in full.

### 3. Pull out the checkable claims

Search the transcript for the facts that can be verified, such as:

- contract addresses, transaction hashes and ENS names
- amounts and dates ("last Tuesday", "block 21 million")
- names of tokens, bridges and protocols
- claims about audits, locked liquidity or burned supply

Copy each one into a note. A transcript is far easier to scan for these than a recording.

### 4. Check them in Web3Lens

Paste every address or hash into Web3Lens. It auto-detects the format and chain, validates the checksum and links to the right explorer. Then:

- Confirm the amount and date match what the speaker said. See [how to find and investigate transactions](/blog/find-and-investigate-blockchain-transactions).
- Run the [risk checks](/blog/blockchain-address-risk-checks) on any contract or wallet mentioned, so a sanctioned or reported address is flagged early.
- Use [batch search](/batch) when a video lists many addresses, such as airdrop wallets or a treasury breakdown.

![SummaryTube summary of a productivity video with sections, bullet points and a transcript panel](/blog/products/summarytube-summary-and-transcript.png "The summary, Q&A and transcript sit beside the video, so you can verify a point against the exact words used.")

### 5. Record what you verified

Write down what matched and what did not. Over time, you will learn which channels and speakers state facts accurately, which is more valuable than any single summary.

## How to treat an AI summary

An AI summary is a map, not the territory. Keep these habits:

- **Verify numbers and names yourself.** Summaries can compress or mis-state specifics. The transcript is your source of truth.
- **Check the timestamp.** Jump to the moment and listen to the exact wording, especially for anything that affects money.
- **Mind the date.** Crypto data goes stale quickly. A summary of a six-month-old video describes a market that no longer exists.
- **Separate opinion from fact.** Summaries flatten tone, so "I think this could" can read like "this will".

> [!WARNING]
> Never act on a contract address you only heard in a video or read in a summary. Look it up first, confirm it on the project's official channels, and run the checks. Impersonation videos that read out look-alike addresses are a common scam format.

## Better ways to use the transcript

The transcript is more than a fallback. It is a searchable record.

- **Find every mention** of a contract, protocol or person across a long call.
- **Quote accurately** in a report, with the timestamp for anyone who wants to check.
- **Compare statements** across two calls to see whether the story changed.
- **Create a reading list** of the links and tools mentioned, then store them in a saved session. See [saving your research tabs](/blog/save-and-restore-crypto-research-tabs-session-manager).

## Frequently asked questions

### Are AI summaries of crypto videos accurate enough to rely on?

They are accurate enough to decide what to watch and to find the right moments, but not to quote or act on. Always confirm numbers, names and addresses against the transcript and, for anything on-chain, against a block explorer.

### Can I summarize a podcast or AMA that is only on YouTube?

Yes, as long as it is a YouTube video with captions or a transcript the extension can read. Long formats such as podcasts, AMAs and governance calls benefit most because the key moments let you skip to the relevant part.

### Is SummaryTube free?

It has a free tier, with a paid plan for higher limits and extra features. Check [summarytube.com](https://summarytube.com/) for the current allowances.

### How do I check an address mentioned in a video?

Paste it into Web3Lens. It detects the format, checks the checksum and shows the matching explorers and risk flags. If the checksum is invalid or the address differs from the project's official channels, do not send funds to it.
